Real-to-sim for robotics

Splats are not scenes

3D Gaussian Splatting is excellent at reproducing photographs of a place. A robot simulator needs something different: separable objects with surfaces, geometry, and appearance that holds up under new viewpoints and edits. This note explains why splats fall short on each of those, with examples from real splat reconstructions.

Yiming Chen · Nov 2026

What real-to-sim actually needs

Gaussian Splatting represents a scene as millions of 3D Gaussians, each with a position, covariance, opacity and colour. They are optimised for one objective: rendered images should match the training photos. Nothing in that loss asks for a correct surface, a separable object, or physically meaningful colour.

A real-to-sim pipeline asks for all three. We want to pick a mug out of a reconstructed kitchen, give it a collision shape and mass, move it, render the scene from a wrist camera the capture never visited, and have the policy see something consistent with reality. Each step leans on a property that splats were never trained to have.

1

Appearance depends on where you look

Each Gaussian's colour is not a single RGB value. It is a set of spherical-harmonic (SH) coefficients, usually up to degree 3 (48 numbers per Gaussian), evaluated against the viewing direction. This is how splats reproduce specular highlights and view-dependent shading. It also means:

  • Lighting is baked in. Shadows, highlights and environment reflections become part of the "texture". Move the object, change the light, or relight the sim, and the shading stays where it was captured.
  • Off-distribution views break. The SH fit is only constrained along the capture trajectory. A robot's wrist or head camera often sits lower, closer, or at grazing angles. There, high-order SH terms extrapolate freely and colours shift, saturate or flicker.
  • Reflections become fake geometry. Shiny surfaces such as the steel ball in Fig. 4 are often explained by Gaussians placed behind the surface, so geometry and appearance are both wrong.
  • Moved objects carry the wrong colour. When an object is rotated in sim, its SH lobes rotate with it, but the light in the scene did not. The object now shows highlights facing the wrong way.
For policy learning this matters directly. Visual observations that change with viewpoint in ways the real camera would not produce add a sim-to-real gap at exactly the point real-to-sim was meant to close.
2

Segmented objects come with a cloud of noise

Object masks for splats are usually lifted from 2D (SAM-style masks projected onto Gaussians, or a learned per-Gaussian feature). Two things go wrong. Large, semi-transparent Gaussians straddle the object boundary and get assigned to one side or the other. And the optimiser leaves many low-opacity floaters that contribute almost nothing to any single training image but add up when the object is viewed in isolation.

A threshold on opacity does not fix this cleanly. The diagram below shows why: along any ray, the rendered colour is an alpha blend of many Gaussians, and there is no single point that is "the surface".

Raise the threshold and the object's soft outer layer disappears, leaving it shrunken and see-through at the rim. Lower it and the floaters stay. Clean-up then becomes manual, per object and per scene, which defeats the point of an automatic pipeline.

3

Removing an object leaves a hole in the world

Real-to-sim usually needs the scene without the object too, so the object can be re-inserted as a simulated body. When we delete the object's Gaussians, the space behind and beneath it was never observed by the cameras. There is nothing there to render. What remains is a smeared, semi-transparent residue made of boundary Gaussians that belonged partly to the object and partly to the table.

2D or 3D inpainting (for example diffusion-based fill) can paint over the gap, but the result is a plausible guess rather than a measurement, it is often inconsistent across views, and it has to be redone every time an object is moved.

4

There is no geometry to simulate

Physics engines need meshes or primitives: watertight surfaces for collision, volumes for mass and inertia, normals for contact. A splat has none of these. Depth rendered from splats is an alpha-weighted average along each ray, which blurs edges and is pulled toward floaters. Mesh extraction methods (SuGaR, 2DGS, Gaussian Opacity Fields) help, but they add a second representation that no longer matches the rendered appearance, and they inherit the same floaters and holes shown above.

Where splats still help

This is not an argument to drop splats entirely. They remain one of the fastest ways to get a photoreal backdrop from a phone video. The practical position is to keep them away from anything the robot touches:

  • Use splats only for static, distant background that no camera approaches closely and nothing collides with.
  • Represent interactable objects as meshes with collision shapes and PBR materials, reconstructed or scanned separately.
  • If splats must be used near the robot, restrict SH to degree 0 or 1, prune low-opacity Gaussians, and capture extra views from robot-camera heights.
  • Evaluate any splat-based sim on held-out robot viewpoints, not on the capture trajectory where it always looks good.

Short versionSplats answer "what does this place look like from here?" Real-to-sim needs answers to "what is this object, where is its surface, and what happens when I move it?" Splats do not encode those.
