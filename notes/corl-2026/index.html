CoRL 2026 · notes from the poster sessions

What is the robot's own piece?

Robot learning got its biggest recent jumps by borrowing: language models became VLAs, and video diffusion became world action models. After walking the CoRL 2026 poster sessions, my question is what comes next. What would a core representation look like if it were built for robots first? My bet is something object-centric and 3D, but not a full simulation.

Yiming Chen · Nov 2026

01

What the posters looked like

Walking the poster sessions, my rough impression was that around 60% of the work followed the same shape: take an existing pipeline (usually a VLA, a diffusion or flow-matching policy, or a world model), add a focused technique to it, and show a gain on the benchmark. Sometimes the gain is small and sometimes it is large.

I don't mean that as a criticism. Most of these are solid papers. Adding force feedback, memory, token pruning, asynchronous decoding or better action tokenizers to a VLA fixes a real problem that someone deploying it will hit. That is how a field gets pipelines that actually work.

The accepted titles show the same pattern. Many VLA papers follow an "X-VLA: add Y to a VLA" shape. Examples include force (FM-VLA, FR-VLA, CompVLA), memory (EventVLA, RAMT-VLA), speed (FlashVLA, EagleVLA, BitVLA, AsyncVLA), and hierarchy (H-VLA, HAF-VLA). The WAM papers do the same thing to world action models: making them lighter (Light-WAM, LaWAM, C³ache), adding force (FAWAM), adding memory (MemoryVAM), or adding masks (MaskWAM). Each one makes sense on its own. Taken together, they show where most of the field's effort is going: refining borrowed backbones.

02

Step back: where the big jumps came from

Looking at the field as a whole, the pattern behind the last few years is fairly clear. Robotics has repeatedly taken a model that another field built beautifully and adapted it to act:

The first two rows were built by other fields for their own data. The third row would be built for the data robots actually act on.

LLMs were built for text, and VLAs inherit their strengths: broad semantics, instruction following, and the ability to scale with data. π0.7 is steered by subtask text and subgoal images, and MolmoAct2 reasons in language before it acts. Video diffusion was built for pixels, and WAMs inherit its ability to imagine how a scene will evolve. DreamZero, a 14B WAM, jointly denoises future video and actions and generalizes zero-shot better than VLAs. Both families do a decent job on robot tasks, and both fit the robot problem only approximately.

Neither was designed around what a robot actually deals with, which is physical objects in 3D: things with extent, pose, contact and permanence that persist when they leave the camera frame. The piece that seems to be missing is a core representation that is native to that.

03

Object-centric, but not a full simulator

Robots work on real-world tasks with objects, so the natural candidate is an object-centric representation. The open question is how much 3D it needs. The two ends of the spectrum both seem wrong to me. Here is the same mug in each:

2D pixels

Images or video frames, possibly with masks, keypoints or affordance maps.

Keeps
Internet-scale data and pretrained vision models. Already solves many tasks
Loses
Depth, extent and occlusion. Objects stop existing when they leave the frame

Object-centric 3D

A set of objects, each with a pose, an approximate shape and relations to the others. It needs to be accurate enough to act on, not to render.

Keeps
The geometry manipulation needs, persistent across views and occlusion
Loses
Exact fidelity, which it does not need. Harder to pretrain at internet scale

Full 3D simulation

Exact meshes, materials and physics parameters for everything in the scene.

Keeps
Everything: physically complete and editable
Loses
Cheapness. Hard to build and keep in sync, and spends fidelity the task does not need

A purely 2D representation misses a lot of 3D structure. A full simulation goes further than necessary, because a robot rarely needs every object to be 100% correct to act on it. The interesting design space is the middle.

There are signs at CoRL that the field is already reaching toward the middle. Mostly, though, it is still being added onto a borrowed backbone rather than serving as the core of the model:

  • Closing the Loop in Humanoid VLA: Persistent 3D Object TokensAdds persistent 3D object tokens to a VLA
  • Box Trajectories as Compact State Representations for World Action ModelsRepresents state as object boxes instead of pixels
  • ORD-WM: Object-Centric Rigid Dynamics for 3D World ModelingA world model that predicts how objects move, not how pixels change
  • DepthWorld, GSWAM and μ0World models that predict in 3D, through depth, Gaussians or 3D interaction traces, instead of pixels
  • EgoWAM: World Action Models Beyond Pixels and ImageWAMBoth question whether WAMs need full video generation
  • GeomVLA, SAGE-3D, Object-Centric Conditioning for Visuomotor Flow MatchingAdd geometry or object structure to existing policy classes

In a way, these papers are the incremental pattern from section 01 applied to 3D: object structure added onto a model that was not built around it. The question is what it would look like with objects at the center of the model from the start.

04

A debate: do robots need 3D?

At the conference I met with Jiayuan Mao, and we spent a good while debating whether future robot models will need 3D at all. We landed on different sides.

Jiayuan's view

Pixel-level 2D representations, combined with enough affordance information, can already learn and solve a wide range of tasks today. If that keeps holding as data scales, explicit 3D may not be necessary.

My view

Humans do not perceive and reason about the world at the RGB level. We think about the geometry of the objects around us: where they are, how big they are, what is behind them, and how they will fit together. A robot representation that is meant to last should work at that level too.

This is my paraphrase of Jiayuan's position from our conversation, not a quote.

I think both points hold for different things. Jiayuan is right about the evidence today: 2D + affordance systems work, and they ride on huge pretrained vision models that 3D does not have yet. My disagreement is about where this is going. Pixels are how the world is observed, but objects and their geometry are what the robot acts on. A model that learns from pixels has to rediscover that structure implicitly, task by task. A representation that keeps the structure explicit, even in an approximate form, should generalize more cheaply across viewpoints, occlusions and embodiments.

Where I expect the difference to show:

  • Occlusion and object permanence: tasks where the relevant object is hidden for part of the episode.
  • Viewpoint changes: the same task seen from a camera position that never appeared in training.
  • Fit and contact: insertion, stacking and packing, where success depends on 3D extent rather than appearance.
  • Cross-embodiment transfer: object geometry stays the same when the robot changes, but the pixels do not.
Short version

Robotics borrowed language models and got VLAs, then borrowed video generation and got WAMs. The next big step is probably a representation that belongs to robots: object-centric, 3D enough to act on, and no more faithful to reality than the task requires.

The papers I read closely at the conference, and the ones I marked to read next. Links go to arXiv, or to OpenReview where there is no arXiv version.
